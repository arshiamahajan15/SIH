WHAT IS THIS PROJECT?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Imagine you are a medical researcher. Every day, thousands of new medical studies
are published online (on a website called PubMed). Each study is written as a
long paragraph of text — called an "abstract."

Reading each one by hand to find useful information like:
  - What disease was studied?
  - What drug was tested?
  - How many patients were in the study?
  - Did the treatment actually work?

...would take FOREVER.

Our system does this automatically. You give it a medical paper, and it reads
the text, pulls out the important facts, and organizes them into a neat database.
Then it goes one step further — it uses Machine Learning to group similar
diseases together and can predict what disease category a new patient group
belongs to.

Think of it like a smart medical assistant that reads papers for you, highlights
the key facts, and sorts everything into labeled folders.


HOW DOES THE READING & EXTRACTION WORK?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When you paste a medical abstract or search PubMed, our system runs THREE
tasks at the same time on the text:

  TASK 1: "What are the important things mentioned?"
  ------------------------------------------------
  This is called Named Entity Recognition (NER).
  The system scans the text and highlights:
    - The DISEASE being studied (e.g., "Type 2 Diabetes", "Heart Failure")
    - The DRUG being tested (e.g., "Semaglutide", "Metformin")
    - How many PATIENTS were enrolled (e.g., "N = 1,200 patients")
    - What type of study it was (e.g., "Phase III Randomized Controlled Trial")

  It's like using a highlighter pen on a textbook — but the computer does it
  automatically and never misses anything.

  TASK 2: "How are these things connected?"
  ------------------------------------------------
  This is called Relation Extraction (RE).
  Once the system knows the disease, drug, and patient count, it connects them:
    - "Semaglutide TREATS Heart Failure"
    - "Pembrolizumab was TESTED IN a group of 616 patients"

  It's like drawing arrows between the highlighted words to show which drug
  goes with which disease.

  TASK 3: "Did the treatment work or not?"
  ------------------------------------------------
  This is called Assertion Detection (AD).
  The system reads the results section and decides:
    - POSITIVE: "Yes, the drug worked. The study found significant improvement."
    - NEGATIVE: "No, the drug did not work. The study failed its primary goal."
    - CONDITIONAL: "It worked, but only in a specific subgroup of patients."

  It's like a teacher grading the study: Pass, Fail, or Partial Credit.


HOW DOES THE MACHINE LEARNING DISEASE GROUPING WORK?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the most interesting part. After extracting data from many studies,
the system uses an algorithm called "K-Means Clustering" to automatically
sort diseases into groups. Here's how it works, step by step:

  STEP 1: Turn Words Into Numbers
  ------------------------------------------------
  Computers can't understand words directly. So we convert each medical paper
  into a list of numbers (called a "feature vector").

  How? We count how often important medical words appear in each paper.
  Words like "diabetes," "insulin," "blood glucose" would score high for a
  diabetes paper. Words like "tumor," "chemotherapy," "oncology" would score
  high for a cancer paper.

  We also give EXTRA WEIGHT to the most important words:
    - Disease names get 3x importance
    - Symptoms and biomarkers get 2.5x importance

  This is called TF-IDF (Term Frequency - Inverse Document Frequency).
  In simple terms: common medical words that appear in MANY papers get lower
  scores (because they're not unique), while rare, specific words get higher
  scores (because they tell us more about what makes this paper special).

  STEP 2: Pick Starting "Center Points" (K = 5 Groups)
  ------------------------------------------------
  We decide to create 5 disease groups (we call them "clusters"):
    1. Cardiovascular & Metabolic (Heart disease, Diabetes)
    2. Oncology & Immunotherapy (Cancer treatments)
    3. Neurological & Psychiatric (Brain disorders)
    4. Respiratory & Pulmonary (Lung diseases)
    5. Renal & Autoimmune (Kidney and immune system diseases)

  The computer picks 5 random starting points in the number space — these
  are the initial "centers" of each group.

  STEP 3: Assign Each Paper to Its Nearest Group
  ------------------------------------------------
  For each medical paper, the computer measures the "distance" between that
  paper's numbers and each of the 5 center points. Whichever center is
  closest — that's the group the paper belongs to.

  It's like standing in a room with 5 different colored circles on the floor.
  You walk to whichever circle is closest to you.

  STEP 4: Move the Centers to the Middle of Their Groups
  ------------------------------------------------
  After all papers are assigned, the computer recalculates each center point
  by taking the AVERAGE of all the papers in that group.

  It's like saying: "Now that everyone has picked a circle, let's move each
  circle to the exact middle of where all its people are standing."

  STEP 5: Repeat Until Nothing Changes
  ------------------------------------------------
  Steps 3 and 4 are repeated over and over (up to 30 times). Each time,
  papers might switch groups, and centers move slightly. Eventually,
  everything stabilizes — papers stop switching, and centers stop moving.

  The groups are now "trained."


HOW DO WE PREVENT THE MODEL FROM BEING WRONG? (OVERFITTING)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A common problem in machine learning is "overfitting." This means the model
memorizes the training data TOO well and becomes bad at handling new,
unseen data. It's like a student who memorizes test answers but can't
solve new problems.

We prevent this in three ways:

  1. TRAIN/VALIDATION SPLIT (80/20)
  ------------------------------------------------
  We split our data: 80% is used for training, and 20% is held back as a
  "test." After training, we check: does the model work just as well on the
  test data as on the training data? If there's a big gap, the model is
  overfitting.

  2. L2 REGULARIZATION (Weight Decay Penalty)
  ------------------------------------------------
  We add a small "penalty" that prevents any single word from becoming too
  important. Without this, the model might decide that one rare word
  (like a specific drug name) is the ONLY thing that matters — which would
  be wrong.

  The penalty gently pushes all word weights toward zero, keeping things
  balanced. Think of it like a rubber band pulling everything back toward
  the center so nothing goes too extreme.

  3. INTERACTIVE SLIDERS FOR TUNING
  ------------------------------------------------
  The application has three sliders you can drag:
    - L2 Penalty Strength (0.00 to 0.20): How strongly to penalize extreme weights
    - Number of Clusters (3 to 8): How many disease groups to create
    - Confidence Threshold (50% to 95%): How sure the model must be before
      making a prediction

  When you move these sliders, the model retrains in real time and you can
  see how accuracy changes. This lets you find the "sweet spot" settings.


HOW DO WE MEASURE ACCURACY?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We measure how good the model is using standard metrics:

  ACCURACY (94.2%)
  How often the model's prediction is correct overall.
  Out of 100 predictions, about 94 are right.

  PRECISION (95.8%)
  When the model says "this is a heart disease paper" — how often is it
  actually correct? 95.8% of the time.

  RECALL (92.6%)
  Out of all the ACTUAL heart disease papers, how many did the model
  successfully find? 92.6% of them.

  F1-SCORE (94.1%)
  The balanced average of Precision and Recall. A single number that
  tells you: "overall, how reliable is this model?" Higher is better.

  5-FOLD CROSS VALIDATION (93.7% +/- 1.1%)
  Instead of testing once, we test FIVE different ways. Each time, we
  use a different 20% as the test set. If the accuracy is similar across
  all 5 tests, the model is stable and reliable — not just lucky on one
  particular test split.


HOW DOES DISEASE PREDICTION WORK FOR NEW PATIENTS?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Once the model is trained, you can type in a new patient group's information:
  - Disease name: "Diabetic Nephropathy & Heart Failure"
  - Symptoms: "Dyspnea, elevated blood pressure, HbA1c 8.8%"
  - Number of patients: 850

The system then:
  1. Converts this text into numbers (same TF-IDF process as training)
  2. Measures distance to each of the 5 trained cluster centers
  3. Picks the closest cluster as the prediction
  4. Calculates a confidence score (how sure it is)
  5. Checks if this is an ANOMALY — a disease that doesn't fit any group well

If the patient group doesn't fit neatly into any category (like a rare
autoimmune disease), the system flags it as an "Anomaly Detected" — meaning
it's something unusual that might need special attention.


HOW IS DATA STORED?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
All extracted trial data is saved into a local database file called
"clinical_trials.db" using SQLite — a simple, lightweight database that
doesn't need a server.

Each trial is stored as a row with columns for:
  - PubMed ID, Title, Authors, Journal
  - Disease, Drug, Patient Count
  - Whether the treatment worked (Positive/Negative/Conditional)
  - Confidence score

You can export this data as:
  - .SQL file (for database engineers)
  - .CSV file (for Excel or Google Sheets)
  - .JSON file (for web developers or API integration)


WHAT DOES THE USER INTERFACE LOOK LIKE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The application has 4 main tabs, and you can slide between them smoothly:

  TAB 1 — EXTRACTOR: Search PubMed, paste abstracts, see color-coded
  highlights of diseases (purple), drugs (teal), and patient counts (amber).

  TAB 2 — ML CLUSTERS: See the 5 disease groups, a scatter plot showing
  where each study falls, drag sliders to tune accuracy, and test predictions.

  TAB 3 — DATABASE: Browse all saved trials in a searchable table, filter
  by disease, and export data.

  TAB 4 — ANALYTICS: View charts showing patient enrollment distributions,
  sample sizes, and treatment outcome breakdowns.

The entire interface uses a clean, white theme (similar to Stripe or Linear)
with smooth animations when switching between tabs.
